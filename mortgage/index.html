Understanding your mortgage

How mortgage payments and extra principal work

A traditional fixed-rate mortgage is designed around a predictable monthly payment. That payment is primarily divided between principal and interest. Principal reduces the amount you owe, while interest is the cost of borrowing the money. At the beginning of a mortgage, a larger share of each payment generally goes toward interest because your outstanding balance is at its highest level. As the balance decreases, more of each payment goes toward principal.

M = P × [r(1+r)^n] / [(1+r)^n − 1]
P = original loan principal   ·   r = monthly interest rate   ·   n = number of monthly payments

Making an additional principal payment changes the balance on which future interest is calculated. Even a relatively modest recurring payment can therefore have a meaningful effect over a long mortgage term. The earlier the additional money reaches the principal balance, the longer that lower balance can remain in place.

For example, adding $200 to a mortgage payment every month may not feel dramatically different from the original payment. Over many years, however, those additional dollars can reduce the outstanding principal sooner and eliminate a portion of the interest that would otherwise have accumulated.

What this calculator shows

The calculator compares your original mortgage schedule with an accelerated schedule based on the additional amount you enter. You can see your estimated standard principal-and-interest payment, your new total payment, the projected payoff dates, the estimated interest savings, and the amount of time potentially removed from your mortgage.

The results are estimates rather than a lender payoff quote. Your actual mortgage may include property taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance, association fees, escrow adjustments, lender-specific payment rules, or other costs that are not included in the principal and interest calculation.

A simple example

Suppose you purchase a $350,000 home and make a 20% down payment. Your resulting mortgage would be approximately $280,000. At a 6.5% fixed interest rate over 30 years, the principal-and-interest payment is roughly $1,770 per month. Adding another $200 each month increases the amount applied to the mortgage while potentially reducing both the payoff period and lifetime interest expense.

Your own results will depend on the numbers you enter. Try several additional payment amounts to see how a small change in your monthly budget affects the projected mortgage timeline.

Common questions

Frequently asked questions

How is my mortgage payment calculated? +
The calculator uses your home price, down payment, interest rate, and loan term to determine the principal-and-interest portion of your monthly mortgage payment. It uses the standard fixed-rate mortgage payment formula.
How do extra mortgage payments help? +
Additional payments that are applied toward principal reduce your outstanding loan balance. A smaller balance means less interest can accumulate over the remaining life of the mortgage, potentially allowing you to pay off the loan sooner.
Does an extra payment reduce my required monthly payment? +
Normally, no. Extra principal payments generally do not change the scheduled payment on a standard fixed-rate mortgage. Instead, they can shorten the payoff period and reduce the amount of interest you ultimately pay.
Is paying extra every month better than one annual payment? +
Making additional principal payments earlier generally provides a slightly greater interest benefit because the mortgage balance is reduced sooner. However, the most important factor is choosing a payment strategy that you can maintain consistently.
Can my mortgage have a prepayment penalty? +
Some mortgages may contain prepayment provisions. Rules vary based on the loan, lender, property type, and when the mortgage was originated. Review your loan documents or contact your mortgage servicer before making a significant additional payment.
